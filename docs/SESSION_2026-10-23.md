## 15. The corpus: grounding instead of quotation

Founder: it is not bad if the philosopher sometimes archaises; his language may be modern in general and some phrases in another style, as people do; no exact quotation and no source reference are needed (the user can look it up);

## 17. The corpus extended (2026-10-23)
Scratchpad build with `ingest2.py` from Gutenberg: all 22 dialogues of Plato in Jowett's translation, four works of Xenophon (Dakyns), the Clouds (Hickie), Aristotle's Politics (Ellis), Poetics (Butcher), Athenian Constitution (Kenyon) and a Nicomachean Ethics of unidentified translator,

- Retrieval, 17 new cases with terms chosen by me: 15 hits by the first count, in truth 14 because one hit was false (the bones case, see below); the plain misses were wording differences in old translations.
- What the corpus settled:
  - **Aristotle's will frees slaves**: Diogenes Laertius gives the will: "Tychon shall have his liberty when his daughter is married, and Philon, and Olympius, and his son", and the boys who wait on him are not to be sold and are to be emancipated when grown, if they deserve it. Verified.
  - **"Fit for cattle"** is, in this translation of the Nicomachean Ethics, "the many are plainly quite slavish, choosing a life like that of brute animals": the sense is there, the word cattle is not.
  - **The founder's story of Alexander sending bones to Diogenes was not found** in Diogenes Laertius, Plutarch's Alexander or anything else here (a search for it matched only an unrelated line about "your bones"). The grounding rule therefore forbids the philosopher to tell it as a memory unless a source is added; the related story of bones thrown at him like at a dog was found earlier in Diogenes Laertius.
  - **The "limited slavery" of the artisan** (Politics, 1260a) was **not found in the Ellis translation** of 1776; what the corpus has is a commentary line in its introduction: "The artisan only attains excellence in proportion as he becomes a slave". The bridge to today for Aristotle should lean on that line and on his definition of the slave
    ("that being who by nature is nothing of himself, but totally another's"), not on the phrase "limited slavery", until a translation containing it is added.
  - The Aristotle voice sample says "I admit that many people are enslaved by conquest, which has nothing to do with nature"; this is not yet checked against Ellis's text.

## 24. What kind of sources the corpus holds, and the separation of editors' material
Founder's question: are we using analyses rather than what survives from the philosophers themselves, or direct testimony such as Plato's and Xenophon's about Socrates?
Answer: the corpus holds only ancient texts in old translations, no modern study or analysis as such. By kind: (1) the philosophers' own surviving works (Plato's 22 dialogues and the Republic and Laws, Aristotle's treatises, Xenophon's Socratic writings, Epictetus's discourses, the Emperor Julian's orations, Cicero's);
(2) direct testimony about Socrates and the others (Plato's and Xenophon's Socrates, Aristophanes' Clouds, Aristotle's remarks on him in the Metaphysics, Plutarch's essay on his daemon); (3) ancient biography and anecdote (Diogenes Laertius, Plutarch's Lives and Essays, Lucian).
What is modern in the files is the translators' and editors' introductions, analyses, notes and indexes inside the same volumes; these are what the separation of text and commentary is about, so that the philosophers speak from their own words and not from an editor's view.
State of the separation: Plato's dialogues are cut cleanly at the start of the dialogue (the introduction goes to commentary); footnotes of Xenophon, Plutarch, Cicero and the Nicomachean Ethics, bracketed notes and index sections are labelled commentary by rule; passages that speak of "Aristotle" or "Julian" in the third person or carry scholarly abbreviations are labelled commentary.
For the scanned Internet Archive volumes (the Aristotle translations, Julian, Dio) the introductions are not reliably cut: a test in which a small model labelled every sixth passage as text or commentary gave patchy results (alternating runs of six passages, whole volumes labelled as text although they begin with introductions)
and was **not adopted**. The working database is `corpus2.db` (rules only); the editors' material in the scanned volumes still leaks in part, and the Politics in Jowett's translation, whose introduction is about 30% of the file, is the clearest case. Tokens used by the test: about 37,000 in, 1,500 out (the small model).
Options to finish: find cleaner plain-text editions of the same translations (Gutenberg or Wikisource), cut each scanned volume at the first line of the translation by hand once, or drop the scanned volumes whose boundaries cannot be fixed. Founder to decide.

## 26. The Greek originals tried (2026-10-23)

  Cost of the test: about 32,000 tokens in, 10,000 out.
- Two things the test showed about the model, both independent of the Greek: (1) without any guidance on length the replies became essays of several hundred words, too long for a chat; (2) the philosophers tend to renounce or apologise for views a modern reader dislikes ("I would not defend that now", "a person reading that cold is right to be disturbed"),
  which is the modern measure the founder does not want them judged by; in an earlier run Aristotle said instead that he meant what he wrote. Decisions pending from the founder on how to handle both (length can live in the output layer; the second is a question of how the frame describes them).

## 27. Greek first, and the shape of an unpopular view
Founder: give priority to the Greek texts; when something is unpopular today the philosopher puts it in the frame of his own time, explains why it made sense then and not now, contrasts what holds today, asks what real freedom is (a philosophical question, not a position fixed thousands of years ago); the principles of philosophy are the weapon of the philosopher and of us.
Done: the Greek corpus was extended with Aristotle (canonical-greekLit: Nicomachean and Eudemian Ethics, Politics, Metaphysics, Rhetoric, Poetics, Economics, Athenian Constitution, On Virtues and Vices; First1KGreek: On the Soul, Physics, Categories, Topics, animals and others): 9,343 passages in all, 44 MB.
Retrieval in Greek for Aristotle (8 cases, the model writing the Greek words): the right passage among the first six in 8 and first in 7. Passages longer than 200 words are cut in pieces of 140.
Answers with Greek first (five Greek passages, then two English as help) and the founder's shape written into the frame: the structure came out as asked (says it plainly, what it answered then, what his contemporaries said against it, a contrast with today). A first run, which told him to use his principles to judge both, ended in a verdict of his own on his old view and was long (about 700 words);
a second run, which told him not to settle today's question for the user and that he is in a conversation, gave about 350 words and ended with a question to the user, as intended. The sources themselves held the dissent of his own time (Aristotle records those who held slavery unjust; Plato's women guardians; Xenophon's household), which is why the shape needs no claim that the old arrangement was in balance with its times.
Cost: about 30,000 tokens in, 8,000 out for these runs. Still open: Greek for Plutarch (Perseus) and Epictetus (First1KGreek), the licence of the CC BY-SA data, and the length of replies, which belongs to the output layer.

## 28. Greek Plutarch and Epictetus, and what CC BY-SA asks of us
Added to the Greek corpus: Plutarch (142 works from the Perseus canonical repository: the Lives in Perrin's text and the Moralia in Vernardakis's) and Epictetus (the Discourses and the Handbook, Schenkl's text, from Perseus; the First1KGreek entries for Epictetus turned out to be only the anthologies of Stobaeus, so they were dropped).
Now 206 works, 16,807 passages, 82 MB. Retrieval tests with Greek words written by the model: Alexander meeting Diogenes (Plutarch, Alexander 14), the divine sign of Socrates (De Genio Socratis), the true Cynic (Epictetus, Discourses 3.22) and Plutarch's essay on the flatterer were all found in the first results.
The explanation of CC BY-SA given to the founder (general information, not legal advice): the licence asks for attribution (the creators named by the licensor, the licence and a note of changes) and for share-alike (an adaptation that is distributed is licensed on the same terms); it allows commercial use.
Our segmented and normalised database is an adaptation of the encoded texts, so it would be distributed as a separate data pack under CC BY-SA; the application code, a separate work that merely reads the pack, can stay ours (the standard approach, arguable, hence a lawyer); the pack cannot be encrypted or gated, since the licence forbids technical measures that restrict users;
the philosopher's replies are new text. Separately, the editions of five Aristotle works are of the twentieth century (Ross 1924, 1957, 1959, Kassel 1965, Clarendon 1960) and need a check of the editor's right. Options and a recommendation are in the reply that went with this section; table of editions in `CORPUS_MANIFEST.md`.

## 29. The corpus shelved
Founder: do not invent memories at all; if the models are already trained on the texts the result is probably the same with lighter, simpler code and no risk; the playful invention of memories was a nice touch but is left out rather than complicate things.
A last check, without any corpus, one model call per reply, the minimal persona plus the shape for an unpopular view plus the plain instruction not to make up an episode (about 3,300 tokens in, 9,900 out, nine replies): the philosophers recalled real material unprompted and distinguished sources correctly
(Socrates, asked about lying, gave the weapons returned to a madman from the Republic and the cases of lawful deceit from Xenophon and corrected himself on which was which; asked about breeding, he said he could not tell how much of the Republic was him and how much Plato, and brought the sons of Pericles from the Meno;
Plato named the rigged lottery and the secret hiding of the weak infants and then the four things he was thinking; Aristotle gave the natural slave and the objection from his own time, then the deformed male and the number of teeth in the History of Animals, and admitted the claim could have been checked by looking into mouths;
Diogenes took the insult, gave Plato's carpets and the exchange about vanity, the cup thrown away at the sight of a boy, and corrected "barrel" to "jar"). No invented episode was noticed by the reviewer (claims were spot-checked from memory, not exhaustively); the replies were long and some were cut by the token limit in the test.
Written into PLAN 18 (which wins over section 13), PLAN header, decision 12 of section 10 and section 7, CHARACTERS_COMMON.md 1 and 4, the sheets, and CORPUS_MANIFEST.md (marked shelved). The scratchpad texts, indexes and scripts remain outside the repository.
